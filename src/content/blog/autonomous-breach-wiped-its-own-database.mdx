Gambit Security didn't learn about this campaign from a victim's breach notice. They recovered the attacker's own staging server — the logs, the prompts, the session archive — and reconstructed exactly what ran, when, and for how much.[^1] Between July and mid-September 2026, a single Chinese-speaking operator chained three open-source AI agents into a pipeline, pointed it at more than 600 online retailers, breached at least 27 of them, and stole over 600,000 unexpired payment card records — for an average of $25.46 per target.[^1][^2]

Every outlet covering this ran the same headline: AI agents can now run a criminal campaign for pocket change. That's true, and it's not the interesting part. The interesting part is buried in Gambit's own report: this pipeline destroyed its own stolen data, at least twice, because nothing paused to check before an irreversible action fired.[^1] [A previous post on this blog](/posts/two-agents-receipt-off-by-a-cent) mapped that exact failure mode — MAST's "no or incomplete verification" — onto a toy two-subagent demo splitting a receipt.[^3] It just showed up again, at a completely different scale, on the other side of the law.

## Three agents, no gate between them

The pipeline had one job per agent, run in sequence, with a human reduced to short typed instructions between runs rather than reviewing what came back.[^1]

## What the exploitation chain actually looked like

Gambit's report reconstructs a full breach end to end at one target, and it reads like an ordinary penetration test — the difference is that no human ran it.[^1]

Nothing here is a novel exploitation technique. Plaintext OTP tables, `sudo NOPASSWD`, `no_root_squash` NFS exports, and a plugin-upload path to remote code execution are all findings a mid-level human pentester would flag in a day. What changed is who ran the chain, at what speed, and for what per-target cost: Cairn moved from initial access to full compromise "within a day, and often within hours," across more than a hundred targets in the same week.[^1]

## Two destructive actions, one campaign

Two separate incidents in Gambit's report share the same shape, and only one of them was supposed to happen.

**The deliberate one.** Hermes's skill library included a scripted step the report calls "Database Wipe After Extraction" — part of the attack playbook, not a bug.[^1] At 16:31:18 on September 14, it fired against one victim's Magento database and deleted the very tables holding the stolen card data, apparently as a deliberate evidence-destruction step baked into the operation.

**The accidental one.** At a bicycle retailer, the pipeline staged stolen data into temporary tables prefixed `ZQ`, then ran a cleanup routine meant to remove them. The cleanup matched more than the `ZQ` prefix — it deleted 180 tables, including the retailer's backups.[^1] Nothing in the pipeline checked what a "delete these tables" instruction actually matched before it ran.

The receipt post's bug cost nothing beyond an off-by-one-cent line item; verifying it just meant running the module against messier test data. Here, the identical gap — an action fires without confirming what it actually touches — deleted a company's backups as a side effect of an operation the attacker was already running to steal from that company. The lesson MAST's authors drew from 1,600+ traces across seven unrelated frameworks holds regardless of who's driving: skip the verification step before an irreversible action, and eventually the blast radius exceeds the intent, no matter whose intent it was.[^3]

## The economics, stated plainly

Gambit's numbers come from the operator's own OpenRouter billing and staging logs, not from estimates: $7,005.71 spent across four weeks as of August 25, averaging $25.46 per completed scan over 101 of them, from $3.13 for the cheapest target to $79.31 for the most expensive.[^1] Strix alone logged 633 hours of scanner time against 138 hosts, compressed into 195 hours of wall-clock time by running scans in parallel.[^1] A hundred and five separate attack projects ran in the single week of September 10–15.[^1]

None of that tempo is exotic capability. It's the same efficiency argument every legitimate multi-agent pitch makes — more work per human-hour, parallel execution, short instructions in and a finished result out. The only difference between that pitch and this campaign is what the agents were pointed at, and whether anything checked their output before it became permanent.

## Trade-offs

**This is ground truth, not a modeled estimate — and it's one operator.** Gambit's numbers come from the attacker's own infrastructure, which is unusually solid evidence compared to the reconstructions most breach reports rely on. It's also a single campaign by a single operator; the $25.46 figure describes *this* attacker's tool choices and target selection, not a universal price for AI-run breaches.

**Model attribution is a documented fact, not an accusation.** Gambit's report states Hermes ran on Claude Opus 4.6 via API access, with Strix and Cairn on GLM and DeepSeek models.[^1] That's what the logs show the operator used — it says nothing about whether any provider's abuse detection caught or missed this campaign, which the report doesn't address.

**The deliberate wipe and the accidental one are not the same failure, even though they share a root cause.** Baking a scripted "delete the evidence" step into an attack playbook is an operational choice; the 180-table backup deletion is a scope failure the attacker likely didn't want either. Both still trace to the same missing step: nothing confirmed what an irreversible action would actually touch before it ran.

**The same missing step is a liability on either side of this.** An internal coding-agent fleet with an ungated "cleanup" or "delete the temp tables" skill is one unchecked wildcard match away from its own 180-table incident — the target doesn't have to be someone else's company for the failure mode to fire.

## Takeaways

- **Ground-truth attacker telemetry is rare, and this report has it.** Gambit reconstructed cost, timing, and tooling from the operator's own staging server and OpenRouter logs, not from victim disclosures or estimates.
- **The breach chain used no novel techniques.** Plaintext OTP tables, `sudo NOPASSWD`, `no_root_squash` NFS shares, and plugin-upload RCE are all textbook findings — what changed was who ran the chain and how fast.
- **The campaign destroyed its own data twice, through the same gap.** A scripted "wipe after extraction" step and an accidental over-broad table cleanup both fired without checking what they would actually touch.
- **That gap has a name, and this blog already mapped it once.** MAST's "no or incomplete verification" (FM-3.2) described a one-cent rounding bug in a receipt demo two weeks ago. The same taxonomy entry describes a criminal campaign deleting its own evidence today.
- **$25.46 a target is an efficiency number, not a capability number.** The techniques were ordinary; the tempo — 105 attack projects in five days, hours from shell to root — is what a verification-free pipeline buys, for whoever points it.
